# AP Chemistry Unit 2: Compound Structure and Properties — Simulation Lesson Plan

Simulation lesson plan · Compound Structure & Properties Sim · 6 class periods of about 50 minutes · Worksheet: “Compound Structure and Properties” Student Notes + Answer Key

## At a glance

| AP topics | 2.1 Types of Chemical Bonds; 2.2 Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy; 2.3 Structure of Ionic Solids; 2.4 Structure of Metals and Alloys; 2.5 Lewis Diagrams; 2.6 Resonance and Formal Charge; 2.7 VSEPR and Bond Hybridization |
|---|---|

| Big idea | How does where the bonding electrons go decide a substance's structure, shape and properties? |

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- Classify a bond as nonpolar covalent, polar covalent, ionic or metallic from the atoms and ΔEN, and predict ionic formulas from charges.
- Read a potential-energy curve for bond length and bond energy, and relate both to bond order and atom size.
- Explain the properties of ionic solids and metals with a particle model, and rank lattice energies with |q₊q₋|/r.
- Draw Lewis structures, use formal charge to choose the best one, and recognise resonance and octet exceptions.
- Use VSEPR to predict electron-domain geometry, molecular geometry, bond angles, polarity, hybridization and σ/π counts.

### Before day 1 (about 20 minutes)

- Run the whole sim yourself once with the Answer Key beside you, so the numbers you see match it.
- Open the sim in Guided mode on the projector and click 1 · Bonds; check that the Evidence panel is readable from the back of the room.

### Key vocabulary

## How this unit runs

**In short:** you show the sim first, students then work through it themselves over a few periods while they fill in the paper worksheet, and at the end the work is graded, by the students themselves or by you.

| Phase | Who drives | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Teacher demo (day 1) | Teacher at the projector | You open the sim, introduce the concept, and walk through the first part of it. Students watch, answer your questions out loud, and fill in the first worksheet page with you. |
| 2. Student sim work (days 2–5) | Students, alone or in pairs | Students run the sim themselves and fill in the worksheet as they go. They record settings, predictions, data and calculations on paper, like a lab notebook. You circulate. |
| 3. Grading and wrap-up (day 6) | Students or teacher (your choice) | Students self-grade with the answer key, or you collect and grade. Then a short class discussion and an exit ticket. |

### Paper lab-sheet norms (read these to students on day 1)

- The worksheet is your lab record. Write in pen or dark pencil, and don't erase: cross out with one line so your first thinking stays visible.
- Fill in **Set up** first and match the sim to it exactly. The answers depend on those settings.
- Write every **Predict** answer *before* you run that part of the sim. A wrong prediction loses no points; a blank one does.
- Copy numbers exactly as the sim shows them, with units.
- Show setup, numbers with units, and the answer for every **Calculate** item, then check it against the sim.
- At the bottom of each page, leave the **Self-check** score blank until grading day.

## Day-by-day plan

### Day 1: Teacher demo: Bonds

**Goal:** Students see that every bond is nuclei attracting electrons, and that the atoms (metal or nonmetal) and ΔEN decide where the electrons go.

**Worksheet pages:** Cover + p. 1 (Bonds)

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Warm-up: "Salt and sugar are both white solids. Why does salt water conduct and sugar water doesn't?" Collect ideas, don't answer yet. |
| 5–35 | Projected demo of 1 · Bonds (script below). Students fill in p. 1 Predict, Guided notes and the data table with you. |
| 35–45 | Pairs finish the p. 1 Calculate box (Al–O, Ca–F) on their own devices or from the projector, then check in the sim. |
| 45–50 | Return to the warm-up: salt is ionic (ions), sugar is molecular. Preview tomorrow: the Energy screen. |

**Demo script.** Do and say this at the projector:

| # | On screen (you click) | You say / ask | Students write |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open the sim in Guided mode and click 1 · Bonds. Point to the Atom 1 / Atom 2 menus, the quick-pair buttons and the Evidence panel. | Every bond is the same attraction: positive nuclei pulling on negative electrons. Today's question is where the bonding electrons spend their time. | First Guided-notes bullet: nuclei, electrons, time. |
| 2 | Click Cl–Cl. Point to "EN 3.16" under both atoms and the ΔEN line (0.00). Drag the pair to the middle and press Check my model. | Same atom, same pull. Where should the pair sit? (Expected: in the middle.) | Cl–Cl row: 0.00, nonmetal + nonmetal, nonpolar covalent, Cl₂ molecule. |
| 3 | Ask the H–Cl Predict question before loading. Then click H–Cl. | H has EN 2.20, Cl has 3.16. Where does the pair go? Why? (Expected: closer to Cl, it pulls harder.) | Predict box 1. |
| 4 | Drag the pair partway toward Cl and check. Point to δ+ on H, δ− on Cl and the purple arrow. | The higher-EN atom gets the partial negative charge. That's a polar covalent bond. | Second notes bullet; H–Cl row (0.96, polar covalent). |
| 5 | Click H–F. Drag the pair toward F and check. Point to H–F on the ΔEN bar (1.78). | 1.78 is past the 1.7 some books use for ionic. Is it ionic? (Expected: no, both nonmetals.) The sim says metal + nonmetal is the better test. | H–F row; fifth notes bullet (continuum, 0.4, metal + nonmetal). |
| 6 | Ask Predict 2, then click Na–Cl. Drag sodium's electron all the way onto Cl and check. Let the animation finish. | Watch the sizes: Na shrinks into Na⁺, Cl grows into Cl⁻. Nothing is shared: an ionic bond. | Predict 2; third notes bullet; Na–Cl row. |
| 7 | Click Mg–O, drag both electrons onto O and check. Read the line above the atoms: "+ and − ions attract" and the Particles line. | Mg gives 2, O takes 2: one of each, MgO. What would Al and O need? (Hold that for the Calculate box.) | Mg–O row (2.13, ionic, Mg²⁺ and O²⁻; MgO). |
| 8 | Click Na–Na. Press Pool into an electron sea, then check. Then click Cu–Zn and do the same. | Two metals: neither wants the electrons, so they pool into a sea shared by every atom. That's metallic bonding, and a mix of metals is an alloy. | Fourth notes bullet; Na–Na and Cu–Zn rows. |
| 9 | Show the Properties to expect line for Na–Cl, then for Cl–Cl. | Bond type predicts properties. We'll test the ionic and metal ones ourselves on screens 3 and 5. | Analyze question 3. |

### Day 2: Student sim work: Energy and Ionic

**Goal:** Students read bond length and bond energy off the PE curve and build and test an ionic crystal.

**Worksheet pages:** pp. 2–3

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Show the Energy keyboard trick once on the projector: click the graph, Home, Shift + →. |
| 5–28 | Pairs complete p. 2 (Energy). |
| 28–47 | Pairs complete p. 3 (Ionic). |
| 47–50 | Checkpoint question to the class. |

**While students work, check for these:**

- Table 1 distances are 430, 124, 74, 54 pm. If a pair has odd numbers, they dragged instead of using Home + Shift + arrows.
- PE is negative in the well (−436), not +436; bond energy is the positive depth.
- Calculate box: break = absorbed, form = released; 614 ÷ 348 = 1.76, not 2.
- On p. 3, the crystal must be complete before the State buttons or hammer work.
- MgO "Dissolved" is refused by the sim: that IS the answer (does not dissolve).

**Checkpoint before they move on:** Breaking 1 mol of H–H bonds absorbs 436 kJ; solid NaCl does not conduct because its ions are locked in place.

**Early finishers:** Explore boxes: compare C≡C with C–C wells; count neighbours in the 3-D NaCl view; hammer KBr.

### Day 3: Student sim work: Lattice and Metals

**Goal:** Students rank lattice energies with Coulomb's law and compare metals and alloys with ionic solids.

**Worksheet pages:** pp. 4–5

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Warm-up: "Which matters more for lattice energy, doubling the charges or a 10% change in size?" |
| 5–27 | Pairs complete p. 4 (Lattice). Hint stays off until the Calculate box is done. |
| 27–47 | Pairs complete p. 5 (Metals). |
| 47–50 | Checkpoint question to the class. |

**While students work, check for these:**

- Calculate on p. 4 is done before Show Coulomb hint is pressed; MgO should come out 5.34× NaCl.
- Rankings written largest first.
- Brass row: + Zn pressed exactly 10 times (Cu 80%, Zn 20%, ≈ 8.60 g/cm³).
- Steel: at least one carbon in a darker hole (rows 2–3 gap) before sliding, or it still slides.
- Radius ratio is small ÷ host × 100 (C/Fe = 61%).

**Checkpoint before they move on:** MgO > CaO > NaCl > KBr; copper stays in one piece when the layers slide because the ions stay surrounded by the electron sea.

**Early finishers:** Rank a set wrong on purpose and read the sim's reason; put carbon only in light holes and slide.

### Day 4: Student sim work: Lewis structures

**Goal:** Students count electrons, build Lewis structures, use formal charge to pick the best one, and spot resonance and exceptions.

**Worksheet pages:** p. 6

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Warm-up: count valence electrons for H₂O and NH₄⁺ on paper. |
| 5–40 | Pairs complete p. 6 (Lewis) in Guided mode. |
| 40–47 | Whole class: why O=C=O beats O≡C–O (formal charges). |

**While students work, check for these:**

- Guided mode, not Free Explore (Explore shows the count).

**Checkpoint before they move on:** NO₃⁻ has 24 valence electrons, 3 resonance structures, N +1 and each single-bonded O −1.

**Early finishers:** Build O₃, OCN⁻ and PCl₅ (Explore box); for OCN⁻, find which structure the sim calls best and why.

## Grading: your choice of self-grading or teacher grading

**In short:** both options use the same answer key and the point values printed on the worksheet. Self-grading is faster and makes students study their mistakes. Teacher grading is more reliable. Pick per class, or mix them.

### Point values (printed on the worksheet's last page)

Each blank or table cell = 1 point · each prediction = 1 point · each calculation = 3 points (setup, numbers with units, answer) · each analysis question and each graph = 2 points. Every page shows its own total in its **Self-check** box.

### Option A: students self-grade (about 25 minutes)

- Students put away the pen they worked in and take out a different color (green or red).
- Project the Answer Key one page at a time, or hand out printed copies to share.
- Students mark each item ✓ or ✗. For each ✗ they write the correct answer next to theirs, plus a few words on why.
- Predictions get the point if they were written down before the sim was run, right or wrong.
- Students total each page in its Self-check box, copy the totals to the Self-Grading Record on the last page, and write the question that taught them the most.
- Collect the packets. Spot-check 3–5 items per packet against the key, and initial the record.

### Option B: you grade

- Collect the packets at the end of the last sim day.
- Grade with the same key and point values. Check the calculations and analysis questions closely; the blanks go quickly.
- Hand back with the Self-Grading Record filled in. Ask students to correct their ✗ items in a second color for partial credit back (a common choice is half the lost points).

### Option C: peer grading

Students swap packets and grade with the key as in Option A, then return them; the owner writes the corrections. The grader signs the record (“A classmate”).

### Pros, cons and risks

|  | Self-grading (A) | Teacher grading (B) | Peer grading (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pros | Fast; students see every mistake right away and correct it while the sim is fresh; no grading load for you | Most accurate; you see each student's thinking; best for a recorded grade | Students read someone else's reasoning; quicker than B |
| Cons | Students may mark generously or copy the key into blanks | Takes your time (about 3–5 min per packet); feedback arrives days later | Uneven grading; some students feel exposed |
| Lower the risk by | Requiring a different pen color, spot-checking, and grading for completion plus corrections rather than raw accuracy | Grading only the calculation and analysis items closely | Anonymous swaps (numbers instead of names); you spot-check |

**A sensible default:** self-grade (A) for the practice grade, then use the exit ticket or a short quiz for the recorded grade.

## Common misconceptions and how the sim helps

| Misconception | What to do in the sim |
|---|---|
| Breaking a bond releases energy. | Energy screen: Pull apart from the well bottom; the PE readout climbs from −436 to about 0 kJ/mol, so energy must be added. |
| Any ΔEN above 1.7 means ionic. | Bonds screen: H–F (ΔEN 1.78) checks as polar covalent because both atoms are nonmetals; Al–Cl (1.55) checks as ionic. |
| Ionic solids conduct because they are made of ions. | Ionic screen: the bulb stays off for the solid and lights only when Molten or Dissolved. |
| Four bonds always means tetrahedral, and lone pairs don't affect the shape. | VSEPR/3-D: XeF₄ (4 bonds + 2 lone pairs) is square planar; NH₃'s lone pair squeezes the angle to ≈107°. |
| A molecule with polar bonds is always polar. | 3-D screen Dipole view: CO₂, BF₃ and XeF₄ have polar bonds whose arrows cancel, so there is no net dipole. |

## Sim cautions: tell students before they hit these

These are places where the sim's text or numbers can confuse students. The worksheet already works around them; mention them during the demo.

- On the Lewis tab, Free Explore mode shows the total electron count automatically. Use Guided mode for the counting questions.
- For SO₂ the sim accepts only the structure with one double bond. If a student builds two double bonds (all formal charges 0), the sim wrongly says the formal charges are higher than needed. Both are acceptable at AP.
- On the 3-D screen, the bond-angle arc for SF₄, ClF₃ and BrF₅ shows exact 90° and 120°, but the readout says "slightly less than." Use the readout.
- The VSEPR predictor marks the ideal angle (like 109.5° for water) as correct for molecules with lone pairs. Remind students the real angle is a little smaller.
- The Metals screen gives every ion core one sea electron, even zinc in brass; the Bonds screen gives zinc two. Don't use the Metals screen to count electrons.
- Small radius mismatches between screens (copper 132 pm on Bonds, 128 pm in the alloy check; carbon 76 vs. 77 pm). Use the number on the screen the question is about.
- Brass density in the sim is a simple average by atom percent, a model, not a measured value.
- Show answer on the Lewis tab gives away the structure. Ask students to check their own first.

## Differentiation

### Support

- Pair a strong reader with a student who needs support; the reader reads the Beaky step text aloud.
- Give the Data table 1 rows on p. 7 with the electron-domain geometry column filled in.
- Allow a VSEPR shape chart for p. 8.
- For p. 1 Calculate, give the hint: find the smallest number of electrons both atoms can agree on.

### Extension

- Explain with the Energy screen why C≡C (839) is less than 3 × C–C (348) and what that says about π bonds.
- Predict the shape and polarity of BrF₅ and ClF₃ before checking in 3-D.
- Predict where KF and CaS would fall in the lattice-energy order, and justify with |q₊q₋|/r (no sim check).

## Exit ticket (last day)

**1.** Classify Mg–Cl, N–H and Cu–Al bonds as nonpolar covalent, polar covalent, ionic or metallic.

*Answer:* Mg–Cl ionic (metal + nonmetal); N–H polar covalent (ΔEN 0.84); Cu–Al metallic (two metals).

**2.** Which has the larger lattice energy, NaF or MgO? Give the reason in one sentence.

*Answer:* MgO (3791 vs. 923 kJ/mol): its ions carry +2/−2, so |q₊q₋| is 4 times larger, and they are also closer (212 vs. 235 pm).

**3.** Give the molecular geometry and polarity of NH₃ and of BF₃.

*Answer:* NH₃ trigonal pyramidal, polar. BF₃ trigonal planar, nonpolar.

## AP-style practice tied to the sim

**1.** Multiple choice. The sim gives these bond energies: C–C 348, C=C 614, C≡C 839 kJ/mol, and bond lengths 154, 134, 120 pm. Which statement is best supported? (A) A π bond is stronger than a σ bond. (B) Bond length decreases and bond energy increases as bond order increases. (C) A triple bond is exactly three times as strong as a single bond. (D) Longer bonds are stronger.

*Answer:* (B). As bond order goes 1 → 2 → 3, r₀ falls 154 → 134 → 120 pm and energy rises 348 → 614 → 839 kJ/mol. (A) is wrong: each added π bond adds less than 348. (C) is wrong: 839 < 3 × 348 = 1044.

**2.** FRQ. (a) Draw the Lewis structure of SO₂ that keeps every atom at an octet, and give the formal charge on each atom. (b) Explain why SO₂ has resonance structures. (c) Predict the electron-domain geometry, molecular geometry and approximate O–S–O bond angle. (d) Is SO₂ polar? Justify. (e) Compare with CO₂, and explain why CO₂ is nonpolar.

*Answer:* (a) O=S–O with one lone pair on S, 2 lone pairs on =O and 3 on –O (18 e⁻); formal charges S +1, =O 0, –O −1. (b) The double bond could sit on either O; the two structures are equivalent, and the real molecule is their average with two identical S–O bonds. (c) 3 domains on S (2 bonds + 1 lone pair): trigonal planar electron domains, bent molecule, slightly less than 120°. (d) Polar: the bent shape means the two S–O bond dipoles don't cancel. (e) CO₂ has two double bonds and no lone pair on C: 2 domains, linear, so the equal bond dipoles point opposite ways and cancel.
