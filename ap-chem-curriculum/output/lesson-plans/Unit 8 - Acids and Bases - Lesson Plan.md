# AP Chemistry Unit 8: Acids and Bases — Simulation Lesson Plan

Simulation lesson plan · Acids & Bases · 5 class periods of about 50 minutes · Worksheet: “Acids and Bases” Student Notes + Answer Key

## At a glance

| AP topics | 8.1 Introduction to Acids and Bases; 8.2 pH and pOH of Strong Acids and Bases; 8.3 Weak Acid and Base Equilibria; 8.4 Acid-Base Reactions and Buffers; 8.5 Acid-Base Titrations; 8.7 pH and pKa; 8.8 Properties of Buffers; 8.9 Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation; 8.10 Buffer Capacity (qualitative only) |
|---|---|
| Simulation | Acids & Bases — ChemSims.app → Simulations → Acids & Bases (chemsims.app/sims/acids-bases): Introduction, Buffers, Titration screens, guided mode |
| Big idea | How does the strength of an acid or base, and the presence of its conjugate, decide the pH of a solution and how that pH responds when more acid or base is added? |
| Time | 5 periods × ~50 min (1 teacher-demo day, 3 student sim days, 1 grading and wrap-up day) |
| Student product | The paper worksheet, filled in by hand like a lab record, then graded |

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- Calculate pH, pOH, [H⁺] and [OH⁻] for strong acids and bases, and explain the log scale.
- Use Ka or Kb to find the pH and percent ionization of a weak acid or base.
- Explain how a buffer resists pH change at the particle level and use Henderson-Hasselbalch to find its pH and range.
- Find the equivalence-point volume of a titration and explain why the EP pH is 7, above 7 or below 7.
- Identify the half-equivalence point as pH = pKa.

## Materials and preparation

- One printed **Acids and Bases Student Notes** packet per student (print single-sided if students will tear out pages, double-sided otherwise)
- One device per student or per pair, signed in to ChemSims.app
- Teacher computer and projector for the demo
- The **Answer Key** PDF, printed or ready to project. Students can't download it: the site blocks the Answer Key for student accounts
- Two pen colors per student: one to work in, one (green or red) for grading
- Scientific calculators (log and 10ˣ keys)

### Before day 1 (about 20 minutes)

- Run the whole sim yourself once with the Answer Key beside you, so the numbers you see match it.
- Run all three screens (Introduction, Buffers, Titration) in guided mode with the Answer Key. Do not use ?mode=explore: from the code, the bottles and steps stay locked in that mode.
- Tell students before every screen: do not move the water slider on Buffers or Titration, and do not move the titrant slider. The Answer Key depends on it.
- The Titration screen needs many burette taps (70 to the EP, up to 115 after). Show students that fast double-taps stream drops.
- The sim doesn't cover 8.6 Molecular Structure of Acids and Bases (bond polarity, bond strength, oxyacids, why HF is weak), polyprotic acids, indicator selection by pKa, or quantitative buffer capacity; use your usual lesson for them.

## How this unit runs

**In short:** you show the sim first, students then work through it themselves over a few periods while they fill in the paper worksheet, and at the end the work is graded, by the students themselves or by you.

| Phase | Who drives | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Teacher demo (day 1) | Teacher at the projector | You open the sim, introduce the concept, and walk through the first part of it. Students watch, answer your questions out loud, and fill in the first worksheet page with you. |
| 2. Student sim work (days 2–4) | Students, alone or in pairs | Students run the sim themselves and fill in the worksheet as they go. They record settings, predictions, data and calculations on paper, like a lab notebook. You circulate. |
| 3. Grading and wrap-up (day 5) | Students or teacher (your choice) | Students self-grade with the answer key, or you collect and grade. Then a short class discussion and an exit ticket. |

### Paper lab-sheet norms (read these to students on day 1)

- The worksheet is your lab record. Write in pen or dark pencil, and don't erase: cross out with one line so your first thinking stays visible.
- Fill in **Set up** first and match the sim to it exactly. The answers depend on those settings.
- Write every **Predict** answer *before* you run that part of the sim. A wrong prediction loses no points; a blank one does.
- Copy numbers exactly as the sim shows them, with units.
- Show setup, numbers with units, and the answer for every **Calculate** item, then check it against the sim.
- At the bottom of each page, leave the **Self-check** score blank until grading day.

## Grading: your choice of self-grading or teacher grading

**In short:** both options use the same answer key and the point values printed on the worksheet. Self-grading is faster and makes students study their mistakes. Teacher grading is more reliable. Pick per class, or mix them.

### Point values (printed on the worksheet's last page)

Each blank or table cell = 1 point · each prediction = 1 point · each calculation = 3 points (setup, numbers with units, answer) · each analysis question and each graph = 2 points. Every page shows its own total in its **Self-check** box.

### Option A: students self-grade (about 25 minutes)

- Students put away the pen they worked in and take out a different color (green or red).
- Project the Answer Key one page at a time, or hand out printed copies to share.
- Students mark each item ✓ or ✗. For each ✗ they write the correct answer next to theirs, plus a few words on why.
- Predictions get the point if they were written down before the sim was run, right or wrong.
- Students total each page in its Self-check box, copy the totals to the Self-Grading Record on the last page, and write the question that taught them the most.
- Collect the packets. Spot-check 3–5 items per packet against the key, and initial the record.

### Option B: you grade

- Collect the packets at the end of the last sim day.
- Grade with the same key and point values. Check the calculations and analysis questions closely; the blanks go quickly.
- Hand back with the Self-Grading Record filled in. Ask students to correct their ✗ items in a second color for partial credit back (a common choice is half the lost points).

### Option C: peer grading

Students swap packets and grade with the key as in Option A, then return them; the owner writes the corrections. The grader signs the record (“A classmate”).

### Pros, cons and risks

|  | Self-grading (A) | Teacher grading (B) | Peer grading (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pros | Fast; students see every mistake right away and correct it while the sim is fresh; no grading load for you | Most accurate; you see each student's thinking; best for a recorded grade | Students read someone else's reasoning; quicker than B |
| Cons | Students may mark generously or copy the key into blanks | Takes your time (about 3–5 min per packet); feedback arrives days later | Uneven grading; some students feel exposed |
| Lower the risk by | Requiring a different pen color, spot-checking, and grading for completion plus corrections rather than raw accuracy | Grading only the calculation and analysis items closely | Anonymous swaps (numbers instead of names); you spot-check |

**A sensible default:** self-grade (A) for the practice grade, then use the exit ticket or a short quiz for the recorded grade.

## Common misconceptions and how the sim helps

| Misconception | What to do in the sim |
|---|---|
| A weak acid has a high pH because it is “dilute”. | Page 2: HCl and HA are both 0.10 M, yet pH is 1.0 vs 2.6. Strength (how much ionizes) is not concentration. |
| The equivalence point is always pH 7. | Page 7: HA + KOH reaches its EP at pH 8.69; B⁻ + HCl at 5.18. The conjugate left at the EP reacts with water. |
| A buffer keeps pH constant no matter how much acid is added. | Page 4: the HCl test drops the buffer from 4.14 to 2.64, below its range. Buffers resist change only until the conjugate base is used up. |
| Doubling [H⁺] halves the pH. | Page 1: 7 → 14 shakes doubles [H⁺] but pH only moves 1.3 → 1.0. |
| Half-equivalence pH = pKa only works in titrations. | Conclusion Q2: the MA buffer (p. 4) and the halfway point (p. 7) both have [A⁻] = [HA] and pH 4.14. |

## Sim cautions: tell students before they hit these

These are places where the sim's text or numbers can confuse students. The worksheet already works around them; mention them during the demo.

- Use guided mode only. The ?mode=explore link does not unlock the bottles or move through steps on these screens.
- On Buffers and Titration, moving the water slider or the titrant slider changes every number. Tell students to leave both alone.
- On the Introduction screen the water slider does not change pH at all; pH depends only on the number of shakes.
- When HA is first chosen on the Introduction screen, the guide says HA “dissociates completely”. That is wrong for a weak acid; point students to the ⇌ arrow.
- After the strong-base graph the guide still says “moles of strong acid you added”. It means base.
- The Titration math panel's [H⁺] box is wrong for a weak acid before the EP (it shows leftover HA). Use the pH number.
- The weak-base fraction-of-species curve is drawn backwards. Skip it.
- HCN, F⁻ and HS⁻ use unrealistic constants (HCN Ka 9×10⁻⁵; F⁻ Kb 1.3×10⁻⁵; HS⁻ Kb 1×10⁻³). For F⁻ and HS⁻ the equation shows the conjugate acid as “F” and “HS” instead of HF and H₂S.
- The buffer “limit” always lands 1.5 pH units past pKa; it is scripted, not calculated from moles of buffer.

## Differentiation

### Support

- Pair students; one drives the sim, one records, then swap each page.
- Give a log cheat-sheet: −log(1×10⁻ⁿ) = n; log 2 ≈ 0.3.
- Allow the x ≈ √(Ka·C) shortcut and show it matches the sim within 0.01.

### Extension

- Use the quadratic formula for every weak acid/base and compare to the shortcut.
- Calculate the pH of the HA titration after 0.100 L of KOH (pH = 4.14 + log(0.035/0.099) = 3.69) and compare to the curve shape.
- Explain why HF is a weak acid even though F is very electronegative (8.6 bridge: H–F bond strength).

## Exit ticket (last day)

**1.** What is the pH of 0.10 M LiOH? Show the steps.

*Answer:* [OH⁻] = 0.10 M, pOH = 1.0, pH = 13.0.

**2.** A buffer has [A⁻] = [HA] and pKa = 4.14. What is its pH? What happens to the pH if a little HCl is added, and why?

*Answer:* pH = 4.14. It drops only slightly, because A⁻ reacts with the added H⁺ to make HA.

**3.** Why is the equivalence point of HA titrated with KOH above pH 7?

*Answer:* At the EP the beaker holds A⁻, a weak base, which reacts with water to make OH⁻ (sim: pH 8.69).

## AP-style practice tied to the sim

**1.** (Multiple choice) A buffer is made with equal concentrations of the weak base B⁻ (Kb = 4.0×10⁻⁵) and its conjugate acid HB. What is the pH? (A) 4.40 (B) 7.00 (C) 9.60 (D) 11.45

*Answer:* (C) 9.60. pOH = pKb + log(1) = 4.40, pH = 14 − 4.40. (D) is the pH of B⁻ alone at 0.206 M; (A) is the pOH.

**2.** (FRQ) A 0.400 L sample of 0.335 M HA (Ka = 7.3×10⁻⁵) is titrated with 0.350 M KOH. (a) Write the net ionic equation. (b) Calculate the pH before any KOH is added. (c) Calculate the volume of KOH needed to reach the equivalence point. (d) What is the pH after half that volume is added? Justify. (e) Is the pH at the equivalence point greater than, less than or equal to 7? Explain. (f) Calculate it.

*Answer:* (a) HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O. (b) [H⁺] = √(7.3×10⁻⁵ × 0.335) = 4.9×10⁻³ M, pH = 2.31. (c) n = 0.134 mol; V = 0.134/0.350 = 0.383 L. (d) pH = pKa = 4.14, since [A⁻] = [HA] (Henderson-Hasselbalch, log 1 = 0). (e) Greater than 7: A⁻ is a weak base and makes OH⁻. (f) [A⁻] = 0.134/0.783 = 0.171 M; Kb = 1.37×10⁻¹⁰; [OH⁻] = 4.8×10⁻⁶ M; pOH = 5.32; pH = 8.69.
