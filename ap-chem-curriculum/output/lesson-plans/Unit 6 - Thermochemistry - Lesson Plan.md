# AP Chemistry Unit 6: Thermochemistry — Simulation Lesson Plan

Simulation lesson plan · Thermodynamics Lab · 5 class periods of about 50 minutes · Worksheet: “Thermochemistry” Student Notes + Answer Key

## At a glance

| AP topics | 6.1 Endothermic and Exothermic Processes; 6.2 Energy Diagrams; 6.3 Heat Transfer and Thermal Equilibrium; 6.4 Heat Capacity and Calorimetry; 6.5 Energy of Phase Changes; 6.6 Introduction to Enthalpy of Reaction; 6.7 Bond Enthalpies; 6.8 Enthalpy of Formation |
|---|---|
| Simulation | Thermodynamics Lab — ChemSims.app → Simulations → Thermodynamics Lab (Parts 1–4; Parts 91–94 are Unit 9) |
| Big idea | Where does the energy go when things heat up, change phase, or react, and how can we calculate how much? |
| Time | 5 periods × ~50 min (1 teacher-demo day, 3 student sim days, 1 grading and wrap-up day) |
| Student product | The paper worksheet, filled in by hand like a lab record, then graded |

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- Use −q_system = q_surroundings and q = mcΔT to find a final temperature and the heat transferred in a calorimeter.
- Explain why temperature stays constant during a phase change and calculate the total heat on a heating curve.
- Estimate ΔH°rxn from bond enthalpies (bonds broken − bonds formed), including a sublimation term for solid carbon.
- Calculate ΔH°rxn from enthalpies of formation and connect the sign of ΔH to an energy diagram.

## How this unit runs

**In short:** you show the sim first, students then work through it themselves over a few periods while they fill in the paper worksheet, and at the end the work is graded, by the students themselves or by you.

| Phase | Who drives | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Teacher demo (day 1) | Teacher at the projector | You open the sim, introduce the concept, and walk through the first part of it. Students watch, answer your questions out loud, and fill in the first worksheet page with you. |
| 2. Student sim work (days 2–4) | Students, alone or in pairs | Students run the sim themselves and fill in the worksheet as they go. They record settings, predictions, data and calculations on paper, like a lab notebook. You circulate. |
| 3. Grading and wrap-up (day 5) | Students or teacher (your choice) | Students self-grade with the answer key, or you collect and grade. Then a short class discussion and an exit ticket. |

### Paper lab-sheet norms (read these to students on day 1)

- The worksheet is your lab record. Write in pen or dark pencil, and don't erase: cross out with one line so your first thinking stays visible.
- Fill in **Set up** first and match the sim to it exactly. The answers depend on those settings.
- Write every **Predict** answer *before* you run that part of the sim. A wrong prediction loses no points; a blank one does.
- Copy numbers exactly as the sim shows them, with units.
- Show setup, numbers with units, and the answer for every **Calculate** item, then check it against the sim.
- At the bottom of each page, leave the **Self-check** score blank until grading day.

## Grading: your choice of self-grading or teacher grading

**In short:** both options use the same answer key and the point values printed on the worksheet. Self-grading is faster and makes students study their mistakes. Teacher grading is more reliable. Pick per class, or mix them.

### Point values (printed on the worksheet's last page)

Each blank or table cell = 1 point · each prediction = 1 point · each calculation = 3 points (setup, numbers with units, answer) · each analysis question and each graph = 2 points. Every page shows its own total in its **Self-check** box.

### Option A: students self-grade (about 25 minutes)

- Students put away the pen they worked in and take out a different color (green or red).
- Project the Answer Key one page at a time, or hand out printed copies to share.
- Students mark each item ✓ or ✗. For each ✗ they write the correct answer next to theirs, plus a few words on why.
- Predictions get the point if they were written down before the sim was run, right or wrong.
- Students total each page in its Self-check box, copy the totals to the Self-Grading Record on the last page, and write the question that taught them the most.
- Collect the packets. Spot-check 3–5 items per packet against the key, and initial the record.

### Option B: you grade

- Collect the packets at the end of the last sim day.
- Grade with the same key and point values. Check the calculations and analysis questions closely; the blanks go quickly.
- Hand back with the Self-Grading Record filled in. Ask students to correct their ✗ items in a second color for partial credit back (a common choice is half the lost points).

### Option C: peer grading

Students swap packets and grade with the key as in Option A, then return them; the owner writes the corrections. The grader signs the record (“A classmate”).

### Pros, cons and risks

|  | Self-grading (A) | Teacher grading (B) | Peer grading (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pros | Fast; students see every mistake right away and correct it while the sim is fresh; no grading load for you | Most accurate; you see each student's thinking; best for a recorded grade | Students read someone else's reasoning; quicker than B |
| Cons | Students may mark generously or copy the key into blanks | Takes your time (about 3–5 min per packet); feedback arrives days later | Uneven grading; some students feel exposed |
| Lower the risk by | Requiring a different pen color, spot-checking, and grading for completion plus corrections rather than raw accuracy | Grading only the calculation and analysis items closely | Anonymous swaps (numbers instead of names); you spot-check |

**A sensible default:** self-grade (A) for the practice grade, then use the exit ticket or a short quiz for the recorded grade.

## Common misconceptions and how the sim helps

| Misconception | What to do in the sim |
|---|---|
| Cold flows from the water into the metal. | Part 1 graph: the metal curve only falls and the water curve only rises; the bubble says heat goes from the metal (system) to the water (surroundings). |
| The same amount of heat gives the same temperature change to both objects. | Iron run: 1652 J lowers the iron by 73.4 °C but raises the water by only 6.6 °C (m × c of 22.5 vs. 252 J/°C). |
| Adding heat always raises the temperature. | Part 2: the thermometer stays at 0 °C and 100 °C while 333 J and 2230 J are added; the bubble says ΔH = Cₚ × (T₂ − T₁) is zero there. |
| Breaking bonds releases energy. | Part 3: breaking H—H costs 436 kJ; only forming H—Cl releases energy (431 kJ). The arrows go up for breaking and down for forming. |
| More heat from the burner makes the reaction release more energy. | Part 4: the plateaus never move with the flame; ΔH°rxn stays −120 kJ/mol. The flame only helps reach the activation energy. |

## Sim cautions: tell students before they hit these

These are places where the sim's text or numbers can confuse students. The worksheet already works around them; mention them during the demo.

- Part 1: the burner starts on the lowest setting (40 °C) and goes back to it every time the metal changes, even when you tap Next to go to aluminum. Have students check that the formula line shows "− 100".
- Part 1: the bottom line reads "− 1652 J = 1652 J". The minus sign is part of −q_system; the number in the box is positive. Read q from the right-hand box.
- Part 1: the bubble says "Pinch the slider" for the sample mass, but the control is a pair of ◀ ▶ buttons. It also says "press play", but the graph starts on its own after the drop.
- Part 1: there are no number readouts for the masses or burner; they show only in the formula line.
- Part 2: every number is for 1 g, but the sim never shows the mass. The heat capacity is labelled "Cw" for every substance.
- Part 2: before melting, the bubble says the liquid Cₚ will be 4.2 J/g·°C for every substance. That is only right for water (ethanol 2.44, acetone 2.18).
- Parts 2–4: the graphs are not to scale. Use the numbers, not the size of the graph features.
- Part 3: some bubbles say "Breakind" (typo) and one says "Forming the ___ bond" with the word missing (it should say C—C).
- Part 3: the HCl diagram draws one 431 arrow although two H—Cl bonds form. The −184 and −85 answers appear right after the last bond forms, so calculate first.
- Part 3 menu lists 6.9 Hess's law, but that link only opens the intro screen. There is no Hess's law activity.
- Part 4: the sim calls the exothermic data set "hypothetical". It is the endothermic set (+15, +20) that is made up; tell students to treat the endothermic set as hypothetical.
- Part 4: before the run, the calculation row shows −205 (the products' sum) in the ΔH spot, and later writes the reactants as "(0 − 85)", meaning 0 + (−85). In the endothermic run, the bubble announces "120 kJ (absorbed)" before the activation energy is reached.

## Differentiation

### Support

- Give the rearranged T_f formula with the numbers already placed for iron; students repeat it for aluminum.
- For Part 2, give a five-box leg diagram (solid, melt, liquid, boil, vapor) to fill in before adding.
- Pair students for Part 3 so one swipes and one records each bond energy as it appears.
- Provide a sign reminder card: bonds broken (+), bonds formed (−), sublimation (+); products − reactants.

### Extension

- Part 1: find the mass of lithium that gives T_f = 40 °C with 60 g of water (algebra with the same equation), then test it in Free Explore.
- Part 2: calculate the heat to turn 18 g (1 mol) of ice at −10 °C into steam at 120 °C, in kJ.
- Part 3: use the C₂H₆ result and textbook ΔH°f values to judge how good the bond-enthalpy estimate is.
- Part 4: write the formation equations for C₂H₆, C₂H₅Cl and HCl and use Hess's law to add them into the Part 4 reaction.

## Exit ticket (last day)

**1.** 60 g of water (c = 4.2 J/g·°C) warms from 20.0 °C to 32.1 °C. How much heat did it gain?

*Answer:* q = 60 × 4.2 × 12.1 = 3049 J ≈ 3.0 kJ

**2.** Why does the temperature stay at 100 °C while water boils, even though heat is still being added?

*Answer:* The energy goes into overcoming intermolecular forces to turn liquid into gas, not into raising the temperature.

**3.** Using ΔH°f values C₂H₆ −85, Cl₂ 0, C₂H₅Cl −113, HCl −92 kJ/mol, find ΔH°rxn for C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl. Exothermic or endothermic?

*Answer:* (−113 + −92) − (−85 + 0) = −120 kJ/mol; exothermic.

## AP-style practice tied to the sim

**1.** FRQ. A 50.0 g sample of aluminum (c = 0.897 J/g·°C) is heated to 100.0 °C and placed in 60.0 g of water (c = 4.2 J/g·°C) at 20.0 °C in an insulated calorimeter. The final temperature is 32.1 °C. (a) Calculate the heat gained by the water. (b) Calculate q for the aluminum and state whether the process is exothermic or endothermic for the aluminum. (c) Explain, in terms of particle motion, why heat stops flowing at 32.1 °C. (d) If the calorimeter were not insulated and lost heat to the room, would the specific heat of aluminum calculated from these data be too high or too low? Justify.

*Answer:* (a) q_water = 60.0 × 4.2 × (32.1 − 20.0) = 3049 J ≈ 3.05 kJ. (b) q_Al = 50.0 × 0.897 × (32.1 − 100.0) = −3045 J; exothermic for the aluminum (it loses heat). (c) At 32.1 °C the metal and water particles have the same average kinetic energy, so there is no net transfer of energy: thermal equilibrium. (d) Too low: heat lost to the room makes T_f lower, so q_water is smaller and ΔT of the aluminum is larger; c = q ÷ (m × ΔT) comes out smaller.

**2.** MCQ. Bond enthalpies (kJ/mol): H—H 436, Cl—Cl 242, H—Cl 431. What is ΔH° for H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)? (A) +247 kJ (B) −184 kJ (C) −92 kJ (D) +184 kJ

*Answer:* (B) −184 kJ: (436 + 242) − 2(431) = 678 − 862. (A) forgets the 2; (C) is per mole of HCl, not for the equation as written; (D) has bonds formed minus bonds broken.
