# AP Chemistry Unit 4: Chemical Reactions — Simulation Lesson Plan

Simulation lesson plan · Chemical Reactions + Redox Reactions Lab · 6 class periods of about 50 minutes · Worksheet: “Chemical Reactions” Student Notes + Answer Key

## At a glance

| AP topics | 4.1 Introduction for Reactions (balanced equations; evidence such as a precipitate, color change, gas); 4.2 Net Ionic Equations (spectator ions, half-reactions); 4.3 Representations of Reactions (particle views, balance scales, half-reactions); 4.5 Stoichiometry (limiting reagent, percent yield, gravimetric analysis); 4.6 Introduction to Titration (redox titration only); 4.7 Types of Chemical Reactions (redox, precipitation, acid–base); 4.8 Introduction to Acid–Base Reactions (partial: classification and neutralization stoichiometry only); 4.9 Oxidation–Reduction (Redox) Reactions |
|---|---|
| Simulation | Chemical Reactions + Redox Reactions Lab — ChemSims.app → Chemical Reactions (Balanced Reactions, Limiting Reagent, Precipitation) and Redox Reactions Lab (Parts 1–3) |
| Big idea | How does a balanced equation let us count atoms, moles and electrons, and use those counts to find an unknown? |
| Time | 6 periods × ~50 min (1 teacher-demo day, 4 student sim days, 1 grading and wrap-up day) |
| Student product | The paper worksheet, filled in by hand like a lab record, then graded |

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- Balance equations by conserving atoms, using the smallest whole-number coefficients.
- Use V × M, molar mass and mole ratios to find the limiting reagent, theoretical yield and percent yield.
- Identify an unknown metal from the mass of a precipitate (gravimetric analysis).
- Assign oxidation numbers and classify reactions as redox, precipitation or acid–base.
- Write half-reactions and a balanced net ionic equation that balances atoms, charge and electrons.
- Calculate an unknown concentration from a redox titration endpoint.

## Materials and preparation

- One printed **Chemical Reactions Student Notes** packet per student (print single-sided if students will tear out pages, double-sided otherwise)
- One device per student or per pair, signed in to ChemSims.app
- Teacher computer and projector for the demo
- The **Answer Key** PDF, printed or ready to project. Students can't download it: the site blocks the Answer Key for student accounts
- Two pen colors per student: one to work in, one (green or red) for grading
- Calculators
- Links: chemsims.app/sims/chemical-reactions and chemsims.app/sims/redox

### Before day 1 (about 20 minutes)

- Run the whole sim yourself once with the Answer Key beside you, so the numbers you see match it.
- Run Limiting Reagent once with 3 slow clicks at 0.70 L so you know what 15 molecules looks like, and see the 7.41 g / 98% result.
- Run Precipitation once and notice the 1.8 g label versus the 3.68 g in the equation box after the first reaction.
- Decide the teaching order in advance (it is the worksheet order): atoms (Balanced Reactions) → moles and grams (Limiting Reagent, Precipitation) → classifying reactions by oxidation numbers (Part 1) → electrons (Part 2) → titration (Part 3), which uses stoichiometry and redox together. Precipitation comes before Part 1 so students have seen a precipitation reaction before they classify one.
- The sim doesn't cover 4.4 Physical and Chemical Changes, Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases (conjugate pairs), or acid–base titration with an indicator or pH curve; use your usual lesson for these.

## How this unit runs

**In short:** you show the sim first, students then work through it themselves over a few periods while they fill in the paper worksheet, and at the end the work is graded, by the students themselves or by you.

| Phase | Who drives | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Teacher demo (day 1) | Teacher at the projector | You open the sim, introduce the concept, and walk through the first part of it. Students watch, answer your questions out loud, and fill in the first worksheet page with you. |
| 2. Student sim work (days 2–5) | Students, alone or in pairs | Students run the sim themselves and fill in the worksheet as they go. They record settings, predictions, data and calculations on paper, like a lab notebook. You circulate. |
| 3. Grading and wrap-up (day 6) | Students or teacher (your choice) | Students self-grade with the answer key, or you collect and grade. Then a short class discussion and an exit ticket. |

### Paper lab-sheet norms (read these to students on day 1)

- The worksheet is your lab record. Write in pen or dark pencil, and don't erase: cross out with one line so your first thinking stays visible.
- Fill in **Set up** first and match the sim to it exactly. The answers depend on those settings.
- Write every **Predict** answer *before* you run that part of the sim. A wrong prediction loses no points; a blank one does.
- Copy numbers exactly as the sim shows them, with units.
- Show setup, numbers with units, and the answer for every **Calculate** item, then check it against the sim.
- At the bottom of each page, leave the **Self-check** score blank until grading day.

## Day-by-day plan

### Day 1: Teacher demo: Balanced Reactions

**Goal:** Students see that balancing means conserving atoms, then balance the other four reactions themselves.

**Worksheet pages:** Cover + p. 1

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Warm-up: "When a log burns, the ash weighs less than the log. Was matter destroyed?" |
| 5–30 | Demo of Balanced Reactions, reaction 1 (script below). Students fill in p. 1 with you. |
| 30–46 | Students balance rows 2–5 of the table on their own devices and answer the questions. |
| 46–50 | Check: what is conserved, atoms or molecules? (Atoms.) |

**Demo script.** Do and say this at the projector:

| # | On screen (you click) | You say / ask | Students write |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Project ChemSims.app → Chemical Reactions → Balanced Reactions (guided). | "An equation is a recipe written in atoms. Today we check the recipe by counting atoms." | Write their name on the cover; turn to p. 1. |
| 2 | Open the menu at the top right and choose CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. | Read the message. Ask: "Which side are the reactants on?" (The left.) | Notes blank 1: reactants / products. |
| 3 | Press Next. The sim says CH₄ has 1 C and 4 H, and O₂ has 2 O. | Ask: "How many atoms are in one CH₄ molecule?" (5.) | Listen. |
| 4 | Press Next twice (coefficients, then "balanced"). | "Stoichiometric Coefficients count molecules. A balanced equation has the same amount of atoms on each side." | Notes blanks 2–3. |
| 5 | Stop. Ask for predictions before any molecules go in. | "How many O₂ does one CH₄ need? Count atoms." Take 2–3 answers; don't confirm. | Predict box. |
| 6 | Press Next. Drag one CH₄ into the Reactants beaker. | Point to the C and H scales. Ask: "Why does the C scale tip?" (1 C on the left, 0 on the right.) | Notes blank 4: each scale tracks one element. |
| 7 | Add one O₂, one CO₂ and one H₂O. | Ask: "Which scales are balanced now?" (C only. H is 4 vs 2; O is 2 vs 3.) | Track the counts in the margin. |
| 8 | Add a second H₂O, then a second O₂. The sim says "Equation is balanced!" | "C 1, H 4, O 4 on each side. Three molecules in, three out." | Table row 1: 1, 2, 1, 2 · C 1, H 4, O 4 · 3 → 3. |
| 9 | Add one more CH₄, 2 O₂, CO₂ and 2 H₂O (all doubled). Read the message, then remove them. | "The atoms match, but each coefficient could be smaller. Balanced means the smallest whole numbers." | Notes blank 5: smaller. |
| 10 | Choose the next reaction, H₂O → H₂ + O₂, and hand over. | "Two molecules become three here. Is that allowed?" Let them find out. | Begin rows 2–5 on their own devices. |

### Day 2: Student sim work: Limiting Reagent

**Goal:** Use V × M, mole ratios and molar mass to find the theoretical and actual yield, and identify the limiting reagent.

**Worksheet pages:** p. 2

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Warm-up: "A recipe needs 2 eggs per cup of flour. You have 6 eggs and 2 cups. What runs out first?" |
| 5–10 | Show the two set-up rules on the projector: slider to the top (0.70 L), and 3 slow clicks. |
| 10–42 | Students run reaction 1, do calc 1–2, then run reaction 2 and do calc 3 and the questions. |
| 42–50 | Share answers: why did extra NaHCO₃ not raise the yield? |

**While students work, check for these:**

- Slider at the very top: the beaker label reads 0.70L. If someone forgot, Reset and redo.
- 3 slow clicks of H₂C₂O₄ = 15 molecules. If their n is not 0.06 mol, they clicked too fast.
- They use 0.0789 M (not 0.08) in calc 1, so they get 7.41 g, not 7.50 g.
- Calc 2 is written BEFORE they select Nitric Acid + Sodium Hydroxide.
- Units on every number: L, mol/L, mol, g/mol, g.

**Checkpoint before they move on:** Reaction 1: theoretical Na₂C₂O₄ = 7.41 g, actual 7.26 g, yield 98%.

**Early finishers:** Free Explore: 30 H₂C₂O₄ molecules at 0.70 L. Predict the theoretical mass (14.81 g), then check.

### Day 3: Student sim work: Precipitation

**Goal:** Identify the unknown metal M from the mass of a precipitate, and write a net ionic equation.

**Worksheet pages:** p. 3

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Warm-up: "How could you find out which metal is in a white powder without a label?" |
| 5–40 | Students run the carbonate reaction, do the calcs, finish the second pour, then the iodide run. |
| 40–50 | Groups compare metals (they differ, by design) and check that every group's molar mass matched its table row. |

**While students work, check for these:**

- Volume 0.700 L; CaCl₂ tooltip says 20 molecules; only 2 pours of M₂CO₃.
- They copy 3.68 g from the n = m/MM box, not the 1.8 g label in the Macroscopic beaker.
- Molar mass is grams ÷ 0.0368 mol, and it lands on 106, 74 or 138.
- Net ionic equation leaves out M⁺ and Cl⁻ and keeps (s) on CaCO₃.

**Checkpoint before they move on:** CaCO₃ 3.68 g → 0.0368 mol, and their M₂CO₃ molar mass matches one row of the table.

**Early finishers:** Predict the CaCO₃ mass if they had poured 3 times (15 molecules): 0.700 × 15/190 × 100 = 5.53 g. Test it after Reset.

### Day 4: Mini-demo + student sim work: Oxidation Numbers

**Goal:** Assign oxidation numbers and use them to classify reactions as redox, precipitation or acid–base.

**Worksheet pages:** p. 4 (start p. 5 if time)

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Warm-up: "A zinc strip in blue copper(II) sulfate turns copper-colored. What happened to the electrons?" |
| 5–15 | Mini-demo of the Redox Reactions Lab (script below), stopping after the first box is set. |
| 15–42 | Students finish p. 4 in Guided mode, then the Free Explore challenges. |
| 42–50 | Class vote: is AgNO₃ + NaCl redox? Students give number-line evidence. Start p. 5 if time. |

**Demo script.** Do and say this at the projector:

| # | On screen (you click) | You say / ask | Students write |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Project ChemSims.app → Redox Reactions Lab → Part 1, Guided. Point out the Guided / Free Explore switch. | "New sim, same idea: we count something. Today it's electrons." | Turn to p. 4. |
| 2 | Choose Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) from Choose a Reaction. Press Next. | Ask: "Zn turns into Zn²⁺. Did it gain or lose electrons?" (Lose 2.) | Predict box. |
| 3 | Tap the dashed box above Zn(s), set the slider to 0, press Check. | "Rule 1: an element on its own is 0." Show what a wrong value does: the sim gives the rule, not the answer. | Notes: Rule 1 blank. |
| 4 | Point at the oxidation-number line and the e⁻ lost / gained boxes. Hand over. | "Up the line means electrons lost. Watch what Cu does." | Continue p. 4 on their own devices. |

**While students work, check for these:**

- They read Rule 4 sums aloud: C + 4(+1) = 0 for CH₄.
- Electron tally for CH₄ is 8 lost = 8 gained; 2 O₂ = 4 O atoms.
- Before-and-after numbers for AgNO₃ + NaCl are the same: no change means not redox.
- Peroxide H₂O₂ in Free Explore: O is −1.

**Checkpoint before they move on:** Zn: 0 → +2 (oxidized, lost 2 e⁻); Cu: +2 → 0 (reduced); CH₄: C −4 → +4.

**Early finishers:** Free Explore ion challenges: MnO₄⁻ (Mn +7) and Cr₂O₇²⁻ (Cr +6). Then preview Part 2.

### Day 5: Student sim work: Half-Reactions and Redox Titration

**Goal:** Balance redox equations with half-reactions, then use the MnO₄⁻/Fe²⁺ equation in a titration.

**Worksheet pages:** pp. 5–6

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | Warm-up: "Al loses 3 e⁻, Cu²⁺ gains 2. What is the smallest number both can share?" (6.) |
| 3–22 | Students finish p. 5, ending with Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ in Free Explore. |
| 22–46 | Students do p. 6: Guided Fe²⁺ titration, then oxalic acid and H₂O₂ in Free Explore. |
| 46–50 | Quick share: what does the first permanent pink mean? |

**While students work, check for these:**

- The Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ equation from p. 5 is done before p. 6 (Part 3 assumes it).
- Endpoint is exactly 20.00 mL: first pink that stays. If purple, they press −1 drop.
- mL changed to L: 20.00 mL = 0.02000 L.
- Fe²⁺ moles = 5 × MnO₄⁻ moles (not ÷ 5); divide by 25.00 mL, not 45.00 mL.
- In Free Explore, titrant stays 0.0200 M and sample 25.00 mL; no New mystery sample until the table is done.

**Checkpoint before they move on:** 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol MnO₄⁻ → 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol Fe²⁺ → 0.0800 M.

**Early finishers:** New mystery sample: titrate, calculate, then check with Show answer. Try a 0.0400 M titrant and explain why the endpoint volume halves.

## Grading: your choice of self-grading or teacher grading

**In short:** both options use the same answer key and the point values printed on the worksheet. Self-grading is faster and makes students study their mistakes. Teacher grading is more reliable. Pick per class, or mix them.

### Point values (printed on the worksheet's last page)

Each blank or table cell = 1 point · each prediction = 1 point · each calculation = 3 points (setup, numbers with units, answer) · each analysis question and each graph = 2 points. Every page shows its own total in its **Self-check** box.

### Option A: students self-grade (about 25 minutes)

- Students put away the pen they worked in and take out a different color (green or red).
- Project the Answer Key one page at a time, or hand out printed copies to share.
- Students mark each item ✓ or ✗. For each ✗ they write the correct answer next to theirs, plus a few words on why.
- Predictions get the point if they were written down before the sim was run, right or wrong.
- Students total each page in its Self-check box, copy the totals to the Self-Grading Record on the last page, and write the question that taught them the most.
- Collect the packets. Spot-check 3–5 items per packet against the key, and initial the record.

### Option B: you grade

- Collect the packets at the end of the last sim day.
- Grade with the same key and point values. Check the calculations and analysis questions closely; the blanks go quickly.
- Hand back with the Self-Grading Record filled in. Ask students to correct their ✗ items in a second color for partial credit back (a common choice is half the lost points).

### Option C: peer grading

Students swap packets and grade with the key as in Option A, then return them; the owner writes the corrections. The grader signs the record (“A classmate”).

### Pros, cons and risks

|  | Self-grading (A) | Teacher grading (B) | Peer grading (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pros | Fast; students see every mistake right away and correct it while the sim is fresh; no grading load for you | Most accurate; you see each student's thinking; best for a recorded grade | Students read someone else's reasoning; quicker than B |
| Cons | Students may mark generously or copy the key into blanks | Takes your time (about 3–5 min per packet); feedback arrives days later | Uneven grading; some students feel exposed |
| Lower the risk by | Requiring a different pen color, spot-checking, and grading for completion plus corrections rather than raw accuracy | Grading only the calculation and analysis items closely | Anonymous swaps (numbers instead of names); you spot-check |

**A sensible default:** self-grade (A) for the practice grade, then use the exit ticket or a short quiz for the recorded grade.

## Common misconceptions and how the sim helps

| Misconception | What to do in the sim |
|---|---|
| The number of molecules is conserved in a reaction. | In N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, four molecules become two, but every balance scale has a green check. Atoms are conserved. |
| Adding more of a reactant always makes more product. | In Limiting Reagent, extra NaHCO₃ just piles up after the H₂C₂O₄ is gone and the yield stays 98%. |
| Oxidation means gaining oxygen. | Zn + Cu²⁺ has no oxygen at all, yet Zn is oxidized: its number goes 0 → +2 because it loses 2 electrons (OIL RIG). |
| A balanced equation only needs equal atoms. | In Part 2, Cu + Ag⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Ag balances atoms but the charge check fails (+1 vs +2) and the reaction stays locked. |
| At the endpoint the flask turns colorless, and you divide by the total volume. | The flask is nearly colorless the whole time; the endpoint is the first pink that stays. The sim's 0.0444 M wrong answer is what you get from 45.00 mL. |

## Sim cautions: tell students before they hit these

These are places where the sim's text or numbers can confuse students. The worksheet already works around them; mention them during the demo.

- Limiting Reagent: clicking a bottle fast can lose molecules. Tell students to wait for each batch to land.
- Limiting Reagent shows M and n rounded to 2 decimals (0.08 M, 0.06 mol). Masses are worked from unrounded values, so students should use 0.0789 M.
- In the Limiting Reagent sim, molarity does not change when you change the volume (only moles do). That is a simplification of the sim; don't use it to teach dilution.
- Limiting Reagent says "the solid H₂C₂O₄" (and "solid HNO₃") although the equation shows (aq).
- Precipitation picks the metal at random, so groups get different answers. All three are in the key.
- Precipitation: right after the first reaction the beaker label says 1.8 g, but the real answer in the equation box is 3.68 g.
- Precipitation, iodide reaction: the PbI₂ numbers are wrong in the sim (it uses 334 g/mol and the wrong mole ratio). Use only the MI grams.
- Balanced Reactions calls the formulas "Empirical Formulas"; they are molecular formulas. The menu placeholder says "Choose a Compound" but it lists reactions.
- The reaction named "Nitrogen Oxidation" actually reduces nitrogen (0 → −3). Use it as a discussion question.
- Redox Part 3 says "You balanced this one in Part 2", but only Free Explore in Part 2 has MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺. Page 5 makes sure they do it.

## Differentiation

### Support

- Pair students: one drives the sim, one records; switch each page.
- Give a fill-in calculation frame: n = V × M → ratio → mass = n × MM.
- For p. 4, let students use the rule list on screen and check one box at a time.

### Extension

- Find the oxidation number of Mn in MnO₄⁻ and Cr in Cr₂O₇²⁻, then write the half-reaction for MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺ in acid.
- Design a titration in Free Explore that ends near 25 mL for an H₂O₂ sample, by choosing the titrant concentration.
- Explain why a wet CaCO₃ precipitate would make the calculated molar mass of M₂CO₃ too low.

## Exit ticket (last day)

**1.** Balance Al(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Al³⁺(aq) + Cu(s). How many electrons are transferred?

*Answer:* 2Al + 3Cu²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Cu; 6 electrons.

**2.** 0.0553 mol of H₂C₂O₄ reacts with NaHCO₃ (1 : 2). How many moles of NaHCO₃ are needed?

*Answer:* 0.111 mol (the sim shows 0.11).

**3.** AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq). Redox, precipitation or acid–base? Give one piece of evidence.

*Answer:* Precipitation: no oxidation numbers change and a solid (AgCl) forms.

## AP-style practice tied to the sim

**1.** A 25.00 mL sample of Fe²⁺(aq) in acid is titrated with 0.0200 M KMnO₄. The endpoint is 16.00 mL. 5Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 5Fe³⁺ + Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. What is [Fe²⁺]? (A) 0.0128 M (B) 0.0256 M (C) 0.0640 M (D) 0.320 M

*Answer:* (C). 0.0200 × 0.01600 = 3.20 × 10⁻⁴ mol MnO₄⁻; × 5 = 1.60 × 10⁻³ mol Fe²⁺; ÷ 0.02500 L = 0.0640 M. (This is the sim's Free Explore Fe²⁺ sample.) (A) leaves out the 5 : 1 mole ratio.

**2.** FRQ. A student dissolves 3.91 g of an unknown carbonate, M₂CO₃ (M is Li, Na or K), in water and adds excess CaCl₂(aq). The dried CaCO₃ precipitate (100. g/mol) has a mass of 3.68 g. (a) Write the net ionic equation. (b) Calculate the moles of CaCO₃. (c) Calculate the molar mass of M₂CO₃ and identify M. (d) Is this a redox reaction? Justify with oxidation numbers. (e) If the precipitate were still wet when weighed, would the calculated molar mass be too high or too low? Explain.

*Answer:* (a) Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s). (b) 3.68 ÷ 100. = 0.0368 mol. (c) 1 : 1, so 0.0368 mol M₂CO₃; 3.91 ÷ 0.0368 = 106 g/mol → Na₂CO₃, M = Na. (d) No: Ca +2, C +4, O −2 and Na +1 are unchanged; it is precipitation. (e) Too low: extra water raises the CaCO₃ mass, so moles look larger and grams ÷ moles is smaller.
