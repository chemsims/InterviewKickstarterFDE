# AP Chemistry Unit 5: Kinetics — Simulation Lesson Plan

Simulation lesson plan · Reaction Rates Lab · 6 class periods of about 50 minutes · Worksheet: “Reaction Rates” Student Notes + Answer Key

## At a glance

| AP topics | 5.1 Reaction Rates; 5.2 Introduction to Rate Law (single-reactant rate laws only); 5.3 Concentration Changes Over Time; 5.5 Collision Model; 5.6 Reaction Energy Profile; 5.11 Catalysis (partial: catalyst lowers Eₐ only) |
|---|---|
| Simulation | Reaction Rates Lab — ChemSims.app → Simulations → Reaction Rates Lab (Zero order, First order, Second order, Reaction comparison, Energy profile; Explore mode from the menu) |
| Big idea | How do concentration, temperature and a catalyst change how fast a reaction goes, and how can a graph tell us the order? |
| Time | 6 periods × ~50 min (1 teacher-demo day, 4 student sim days, 1 grading and wrap-up day) |
| Student product | The paper worksheet, filled in by hand like a lab record, then graded |

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- Calculate k and t½ for zero, first and second order reactions from concentration–time data.
- Identify reaction order from which plot ([A], ln[A] or 1/[A] vs. t) is linear, and get k from its slope.
- Explain with collision theory and an energy diagram why temperature and catalysts increase k.
- Use the two-temperature Arrhenius equation to compare rate constants.

## How this unit runs

**In short:** you show the sim first, students then work through it themselves over a few periods while they fill in the paper worksheet, and at the end the work is graded, by the students themselves or by you.

| Phase | Who drives | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Teacher demo (day 1) | Teacher at the projector | You open the sim, introduce the concept, and walk through the first part of it. Students watch, answer your questions out loud, and fill in the first worksheet page with you. |
| 2. Student sim work (days 2–5) | Students, alone or in pairs | Students run the sim themselves and fill in the worksheet as they go. They record settings, predictions, data and calculations on paper, like a lab notebook. You circulate. |
| 3. Grading and wrap-up (day 6) | Students or teacher (your choice) | Students self-grade with the answer key, or you collect and grade. Then a short class discussion and an exit ticket. |

### Paper lab-sheet norms (read these to students on day 1)

- The worksheet is your lab record. Write in pen or dark pencil, and don't erase: cross out with one line so your first thinking stays visible.
- Fill in **Set up** first and match the sim to it exactly. The answers depend on those settings.
- Write every **Predict** answer *before* you run that part of the sim. A wrong prediction loses no points; a blank one does.
- Copy numbers exactly as the sim shows them, with units.
- Show setup, numbers with units, and the answer for every **Calculate** item, then check it against the sim.
- At the bottom of each page, leave the **Self-check** score blank until grading day.

| 35–45 | Students finish the run 1 table, graph and first Analyze question in pairs. |

**Demo script.** Do and say this at the projector:

## Grading: your choice of self-grading or teacher grading

**In short:** both options use the same answer key and the point values printed on the worksheet. Self-grading is faster and makes students study their mistakes. Teacher grading is more reliable. Pick per class, or mix them.

### Point values (printed on the worksheet's last page)

Each blank or table cell = 1 point · each prediction = 1 point · each calculation = 3 points (setup, numbers with units, answer) · each analysis question and each graph = 2 points. Every page shows its own total in its **Self-check** box.

### Option A: students self-grade (about 25 minutes)

- Students put away the pen they worked in and take out a different color (green or red).
- Project the Answer Key one page at a time, or hand out printed copies to share.
- Students mark each item ✓ or ✗. For each ✗ they write the correct answer next to theirs, plus a few words on why.
- Predictions get the point if they were written down before the sim was run, right or wrong.
- Students total each page in its Self-check box, copy the totals to the Self-Grading Record on the last page, and write the question that taught them the most.
- Collect the packets. Spot-check 3–5 items per packet against the key, and initial the record.

### Option B: you grade

- Collect the packets at the end of the last sim day.
- Grade with the same key and point values. Check the calculations and analysis questions closely; the blanks go quickly.
- Hand back with the Self-Grading Record filled in. Ask students to correct their ✗ items in a second color for partial credit back (a common choice is half the lost points).

### Option C: peer grading

Students swap packets and grade with the key as in Option A, then return them; the owner writes the corrections. The grader signs the record (“A classmate”).

### Pros, cons and risks

|  | Self-grading (A) | Teacher grading (B) | Peer grading (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pros | Fast; students see every mistake right away and correct it while the sim is fresh; no grading load for you | Most accurate; you see each student's thinking; best for a recorded grade | Students read someone else's reasoning; quicker than B |
| Cons | Students may mark generously or copy the key into blanks | Takes your time (about 3–5 min per packet); feedback arrives days later | Uneven grading; some students feel exposed |
| Lower the risk by | Requiring a different pen color, spot-checking, and grading for completion plus corrections rather than raw accuracy | Grading only the calculation and analysis items closely | Anonymous swaps (numbers instead of names); you spot-check |

**A sensible default:** self-grade (A) for the practice grade, then use the exit ticket or a short quiz for the recorded grade.

## Common misconceptions and how the sim helps

| Misconception | What to do in the sim |
|---|---|
| The rate constant k changes as the reactant is used up. | On First order, k stays 0.139 s⁻¹ in the panel while scrubbing changes [A]. The rate (k[A]) falls; k does not. |
| Every reaction has a constant half-life. | Compare p. 2 (5 s and 5 s) with p. 3 (3.33 s then 6.67 s). Only first order is constant. |
| Zero order means the reaction doesn't happen or has no rate. | Zero order run 1: rate = k = 0.05 M/s the whole time; [A] still falls 0.15 M every 3 s. |
| Heating lowers the activation energy. | On Energy profile, the panel's Eₐ stays 9e3 J when the burner changes; only the horizontal kinetic energy line moves up. |
| A catalyst gives molecules more energy. | After a catalyst drops in at 425 K the kinetic energy line does not move; the hump gets lower (orange vs. gray). |

## Sim cautions: tell students before they hit these

These are places where the sim's text or numbers can confuse students. The worksheet already works around them; mention them during the demo.

- Values carry from screen to screen. Have students follow the Set Up boxes in order; a reload resets everything to the defaults.
- The arrows are the only way to change values: 0.10 M and 1 s per click on the order screens, 25 K per click on the burner. The pointer marks on the sliders cannot be dragged.
- The sim says C to D and E to F have a "fixed rate constant", but they don't: k is always worked out from the student's settings. The message "You picked a reaction with a rate constant…" repeats the old k.
- Zero order step text says the rate is "variable". For zero order it is constant.
- On First and Second order, the time students set is used as the reaction length in the panel, but the graph runs from that time to a second, hidden time. They only agree when the hidden time is twice the first, which is why the worksheet uses 10 s and 20 s.
- First order: the equations and the graph label say "In" (capital i) for ln, and the half-life text says "In(1)/k"; the number shown is ln 2/k. The rate number in the bottom row stays at the starting rate while the [A] beside it changes as you scrub.
- Second order: the small 1/[A] box shows the reactant line going down; a real 1/[A] plot goes up with slope k.
- Reaction comparison: the cards' k values (0.07, 0.07, 0.14) are fixed and do not match the graphs, and are not "from the previous reactions". The first order card is missing the minus sign in e^(−kt), and the second order card is missing the "/" in [A]₀/([A]₀kt + 1).
- Energy profile step 9 reads "Eₐ is 9J and k is -0.16 when the temperature is undefinedK". Tell students to skip that sentence. Step 18 says k = 1.3 at 400 K, which the panel does not show.
- Energy profile: the text says each catalyst lowers Eₐ (to 8000 J, 6000 J), but the panel keeps Eₐ = 9e3 J and the diagram's lowered hump is the same for all three catalysts. The beaker always finishes, even when the kinetic energy line is below the hump; its speed depends only on the burner.
- Energy profile: choosing "First Order Reactions" draws products above reactants (endothermic), but the text still calls it exothermic. Use Zero Order Reactions.
- The values button shows "-" for a time of 0 s.

## Differentiation

### Support

- Pair students: one clicks, one records; switch each screen.
- Give a half-filled ln and 1/[A] column (first row done) on pp. 2–3.
- Allow t½ to be read from the tables (the time when [A] is half) before using formulas.

### Extension

- Explain why the panel's k on First order would be wrong if the hidden second time were 15 s instead of 20 s.
- Use the catalyst data: if Eₐ really dropped from 9000 J to 6000 J, how many times larger should k be at 550 K? (e^((3000/8.31)/550) ≈ 1.93)
- Write a two-step mechanism whose slow step gives rate = k[A]² for A → B.

## Exit ticket (last day)

**1.** A first order reaction has k = 0.139 s⁻¹. How long does it take [A] to fall from 0.80 M to 0.40 M? From 0.40 M to 0.20 M?

*Answer:* 5.0 s both times (t½ = ln 2 / 0.139).

**2.** For a second order reaction, which graph is a straight line, and what is its slope?

*Answer:* 1/[A] vs. t; slope = +k.

**3.** Name one way to increase k and explain it with the energy diagram.

*Answer:* Raise T (more molecules have energy above Eₐ: the kinetic energy line rises) or add a catalyst (lower hump, smaller Eₐ).

## AP-style practice tied to the sim

**1.** A → B is first order. [A] falls from 0.60 M to 0.15 M in 10 s. (a) What is the half-life? (b) What is k? (c) What is [A] after 15 s?

*Answer:* (a) 0.60 → 0.30 → 0.15 is two half-lives in 10 s, so t½ = 5.0 s. (b) k = ln 2 / 5.0 s = 0.139 s⁻¹. (c) 15 s is three half-lives: 0.60/8 = 0.075 M.

**2.** FRQ. For A → B, a student records: t (s) = 0, 2, 5, 8, 10; [A] (M) = 1.00, 0.63, 0.40, 0.29, 0.25. (a) Show that the reaction is second order. (b) Find k with units. (c) Calculate the rate when [A] = 0.50 M. (d) Calculate the first half-life and explain why the second half-life is longer. (e) A proposed mechanism is: step 1, A + A → A₂ (slow); step 2, A₂ → 2B (fast). Is it consistent with the rate law? Explain.

*Answer:* (a) 1/[A] = 1.00, 1.59, 2.50, 3.45, 4.00 M⁻¹ rises linearly with t (ln[A] does not), so second order. (b) slope = (4.00 − 1.00)/10 = 0.30 M⁻¹s⁻¹. (c) rate = 0.30 × (0.50)² = 0.075 M/s. (d) t½ = 1/(k[A]₀) = 1/(0.30 × 1.00) = 3.3 s; the next t½ = 1/(0.30 × 0.50) = 6.7 s, longer because t½ depends on 1/[A] and [A] is now smaller. (e) Yes: the slow step is bimolecular in A, so rate = k[A]², and adding the steps gives 2A → 2B (A → B).

**3.** Eₐ = 9000 J/mol. By what factor does k increase from 400 K to 550 K? (R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹K⁻¹)

*Answer:* ln(k₂/k₁) = (9000/8.31)(1/400 − 1/550) = 0.738; k₂/k₁ = 2.09.
