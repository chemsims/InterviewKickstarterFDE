# AP Chemistry Unit 7: Equilibrium — Simulation Lesson Plan

Simulation lesson plan · Equilibrium Lab · 6 class periods of about 50 minutes · Worksheet: “Equilibrium” Student Notes + Answer Key

| AP topics | 7.1 Introduction to Equilibrium; 7.2 Direction of Reversible Reactions; 7.3 Reaction Quotient and Equilibrium Constant; 7.4 Calculating the Equilibrium Constant; 7.5 Magnitude of the Equilibrium Constant (partly: K is 10, 1.0 and 0.10 only); 7.7 Calculating Equilibrium Concentrations; 7.8 Representations of Equilibrium; 7.9 Introduction to Le Châtelier's Principle; 7.10 Reaction Quotient and Le Châtelier's Principle; 7.11 Introduction to Solubility Equilibria; 7.12 Common-Ion Effect; 7.13 pH and Solubility (qualitative only) |

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- Write Q and K (Kc, Kp, Ksp) expressions, leaving out pure solids.
- Compare Q with K to predict the direction of a reaction.
- Use ICE tables to find equilibrium concentrations and partial pressures.
- Explain shifts from concentration, volume and temperature changes with Le Châtelier's principle and Q.
- Calculate molar solubility from Ksp, with and without a common ion, and explain the effect of pH.

## How this unit runs

**In short:** you show the sim first, students then work through it themselves over a few periods while they fill in the paper worksheet, and at the end the work is graded, by the students themselves or by you.

| Phase | Who drives | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Teacher demo (day 1) | Teacher at the projector | You open the sim, introduce the concept, and walk through the first part of it. Students watch, answer your questions out loud, and fill in the first worksheet page with you. |
| 2. Student sim work (days 2–5) | Students, alone or in pairs | Students run the sim themselves and fill in the worksheet as they go. They record settings, predictions, data and calculations on paper, like a lab notebook. You circulate. |
| 3. Grading and wrap-up (day 6) | Students or teacher (your choice) | Students self-grade with the answer key, or you collect and grade. Then a short class discussion and an exit ticket. |

### Paper lab-sheet norms (read these to students on day 1)

- The worksheet is your lab record. Write in pen or dark pencil, and don't erase: cross out with one line so your first thinking stays visible.
- Fill in **Set up** first and match the sim to it exactly. The answers depend on those settings.
- Write every **Predict** answer *before* you run that part of the sim. A wrong prediction loses no points; a blank one does.
- Copy numbers exactly as the sim shows them, with units.
- Show setup, numbers with units, and the answer for every **Calculate** item, then check it against the sim.
- At the bottom of each page, leave the **Self-check** score blank until grading day.

## Grading: your choice of self-grading or teacher grading

**In short:** both options use the same answer key and the point values printed on the worksheet. Self-grading is faster and makes students study their mistakes. Teacher grading is more reliable. Pick per class, or mix them.

### Point values (printed on the worksheet's last page)

Each blank or table cell = 1 point · each prediction = 1 point · each calculation = 3 points (setup, numbers with units, answer) · each analysis question and each graph = 2 points. Every page shows its own total in its **Self-check** box.

### Option A: students self-grade (about 25 minutes)

- Students put away the pen they worked in and take out a different color (green or red).
- Project the Answer Key one page at a time, or hand out printed copies to share.
- Students mark each item ✓ or ✗. For each ✗ they write the correct answer next to theirs, plus a few words on why.
- Predictions get the point if they were written down before the sim was run, right or wrong.
- Students total each page in its Self-check box, copy the totals to the Self-Grading Record on the last page, and write the question that taught them the most.
- Collect the packets. Spot-check 3–5 items per packet against the key, and initial the record.

### Option B: you grade

- Collect the packets at the end of the last sim day.
- Grade with the same key and point values. Check the calculations and analysis questions closely; the blanks go quickly.
- Hand back with the Self-Grading Record filled in. Ask students to correct their ✗ items in a second color for partial credit back (a common choice is half the lost points).

### Option C: peer grading

Students swap packets and grade with the key as in Option A, then return them; the owner writes the corrections. The grader signs the record (“A classmate”).

### Pros, cons and risks

|  | Self-grading (A) | Teacher grading (B) | Peer grading (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pros | Fast; students see every mistake right away and correct it while the sim is fresh; no grading load for you | Most accurate; you see each student's thinking; best for a recorded grade | Students read someone else's reasoning; quicker than B |
| Cons | Students may mark generously or copy the key into blanks | Takes your time (about 3–5 min per packet); feedback arrives days later | Uneven grading; some students feel exposed |
| Lower the risk by | Requiring a different pen color, spot-checking, and grading for completion plus corrections rather than raw accuracy | Grading only the calculation and analysis items closely | Anonymous swaps (numbers instead of names); you spot-check |

**A sensible default:** self-grade (A) for the practice grade, then use the exit ticket or a short quiz for the recorded grade.

## Common misconceptions and how the sim helps

| Misconception | What to do in the sim |
|---|---|
| At equilibrium the reaction stops. | The sim says both reactions run at the same rate; the concentration lines go flat but the beaker particles keep moving. |
| At equilibrium the reactant and product concentrations are equal. | Le Chatelier ends at [A] = 0.11, [C] = 0.28; the sim says they "are not equal, just constant." |
| Adding product (or changing pressure) changes K. | The K line and the Q = K panel stay at 10 after adding C and D or squeezing the gas; only heating changes Kp (10 → 1.0). |
| Adding more solid makes more of it dissolve. | After 1875 mg, extra AB(s) sits on the bottom and Q stays at Ksp = 0.10. |
| Ksp is the concentration of the dissolved ions. | Ksp = 0.10, but each ion is 0.32 M: Ksp is the product [A⁺][B⁻]. |

## Sim cautions: tell students before they hit these

These are places where the sim's text or numbers can confuse students. The worksheet already works around them; mention them during the demo.

- Every module starts from its own saved state. The reaction chosen in the first module does not carry into Reaction Quotient, and Pressure/Temperature Changes start from saved numbers, not the students' own.
- After raising the temperature, one instruction box says "the forward reaction is being favored." That's wrong: heat is a product here, so the reverse reaction is favored (the next box and the graphs show the reverse).
- When the volume is increased, the box still says "the system will consume moles to relieve pressure." With a bigger volume, the system makes more moles of gas.
- The flame slider starts in the middle, but the sim treats that as no heat. Tell students to drag it all the way right.
- The Q panel can show a 3-digit number for the second product (like 0.214) because it is adjusted to make Q round right. Copy numbers from the Table tab.
- The Table tab gives away the equilibrium answer right away; calculate first.
- In pH Effect, the Table tab still shows the common-ion numbers, not the pH result; use the Q panel. The red dot on the pH curve slides sideways, which does not mean the solubility stayed the same.
- The 1875 mg and 1375 mg in the text don't match the molar solubility ratio; compare molarities, not milligrams.

## Exit ticket (last day)

**1.** For A + B ⇌ C + D (K = 10), a mix has [A] = [B] = 0.10 M and [C] = [D] = 0.50 M. Which way will it go?

*Answer:* Q = 0.25/0.010 = 25 > 10, so reverse (toward A and B).

**2.** For the exothermic reaction 3A + 4B ⇌ C + 2D, give one change that shifts it to the right without changing Kp.

*Answer:* Decrease the volume (increase pressure), or add A or B, or remove C or D.

**3.** Ksp of AB = 0.10. Is AB more or less soluble in a solution that already has 0.12 M B⁻? Give the molar solubility.

*Answer:* Less soluble: s = 0.26 M (vs. 0.32 M in pure water).

## AP-style practice tied to the sim

**1.** The sim's reaction 3A(g) + 4B(g) ⇌ C(g) + 2D(g) + heat is at equilibrium with Kp = 10 and P_A = 0.74, P_B = 0.36, P_C = 0.25, P_D = 0.51 atm. (a) Show that the system is at equilibrium. (b) The volume is cut to 0.48 of its value at constant T. Calculate Q and predict the shift. (c) Instead, the temperature is raised. State whether Kp increases or decreases, and justify. (d) Explain why adding a catalyst would not change the equilibrium pressures.

*Answer:* (a) Q = 0.25(0.51)²/(0.74³·0.36⁴) = 0.0650/0.00681 ≈ 9.6 ≈ 10 (rounded data), so Q ≈ Kp. (b) Each P × 2.08: Q = 10 × 2.08⁻⁴ = 0.53 < Kp → shifts right (fewer gas moles, 3 vs. 7). (c) Kp decreases: the forward reaction is exothermic, so adding heat favors the reverse (the sim drops Kp from 10 to 1.0). (d) A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally; it does not change Kp or Q, so the system reaches the same equilibrium faster.

**2.** Multiple choice: A saturated solution of AB(s) ⇌ A⁺(aq) + B⁻(aq) (Ksp = 0.10) has extra solid on the bottom. Which change will dissolve more AB? (A) Add more AB(s). (B) Add XB, a soluble salt with B⁻. (C) Add H⁺, which reacts with B⁻ to form HB. (D) Add more AB(s) and stir.

*Answer:* (C). Removing B⁻ makes Q < Ksp, so more solid dissolves (in the sim, [A⁺] rises from 0.26 to 0.40 M). (A) and (D) do not change Q; (B) adds a common ion and lowers solubility.
