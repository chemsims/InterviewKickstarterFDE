## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- Identify the intermolecular forces between particles and use them to rank boiling point, vapor pressure and solubility.
- Classify solids as ionic, covalent network, molecular or metallic and explain their properties from particle structure.
- Use PV = nRT, the combined gas laws and Dalton's law with the sim's R value, and explain deviations with Z.
- Relate temperature to average kinetic energy and read a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution.
- Calculate molarity, moles and particle counts, and draw particle diagrams of solutions.
- Choose a separation method (filtration, chromatography, distillation) from the intermolecular forces involved.
- Connect spectral regions to molecular motions, calculate photon energy with E = hν = hc/λ, and use A = εbc.

## How this unit runs

**In short:** you show the sim first, students then work through it themselves over a few periods while they fill in the paper worksheet, and at the end the work is graded, by the students themselves or by you.

| Phase | Who drives | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Teacher demo (day 1) | Teacher at the projector | You open the sim, introduce the concept, and walk through the first part of it. Students watch, answer your questions out loud, and fill in the first worksheet page with you. |
| 2. Student sim work (days 2–5) | Students, alone or in pairs | Students run the sim themselves and fill in the worksheet as they go. They record settings, predictions, data and calculations on paper, like a lab notebook. You circulate. |
| 3. Grading and wrap-up (day 6) | Students or teacher (your choice) | Students self-grade with the answer key, or you collect and grade. Then a short class discussion and an exit ticket. |

### Paper lab-sheet norms (read these to students on day 1)

- The worksheet is your lab record. Write in pen or dark pencil, and don't erase: cross out with one line so your first thinking stays visible.
- Fill in **Set up** first and match the sim to it exactly. The answers depend on those settings.
- Write every **Predict** answer *before* you run that part of the sim. A wrong prediction loses no points; a blank one does.
- Copy numbers exactly as the sim shows them, with units.
- Show setup, numbers with units, and the answer for every **Calculate** item, then check it against the sim.
- At the bottom of each page, leave the **Self-check** score blank until grading day.

## Grading: your choice of self-grading or teacher grading

**In short:** both options use the same answer key and the point values printed on the worksheet. Self-grading is faster and makes students study their mistakes. Teacher grading is more reliable. Pick per class, or mix them.

### Point values (printed on the worksheet's last page)

Each blank or table cell = 1 point · each prediction = 1 point · each calculation = 3 points (setup, numbers with units, answer) · each analysis question and each graph = 2 points. Every page shows its own total in its **Self-check** box.

### Option A: students self-grade (about 25 minutes)

- Students put away the pen they worked in and take out a different color (green or red).
- Project the Answer Key one page at a time, or hand out printed copies to share.
- Students mark each item ✓ or ✗. For each ✗ they write the correct answer next to theirs, plus a few words on why.
- Predictions get the point if they were written down before the sim was run, right or wrong.
- Students total each page in its Self-check box, copy the totals to the Self-Grading Record on the last page, and write the question that taught them the most.
- Collect the packets. Spot-check 3–5 items per packet against the key, and initial the record.

### Option B: you grade

- Collect the packets at the end of the last sim day.
- Grade with the same key and point values. Check the calculations and analysis questions closely; the blanks go quickly.
- Hand back with the Self-Grading Record filled in. Ask students to correct their ✗ items in a second color for partial credit back (a common choice is half the lost points).

### Option C: peer grading

Students swap packets and grade with the key as in Option A, then return them; the owner writes the corrections. The grader signs the record (“A classmate”).

### Pros, cons and risks

|  | Self-grading (A) | Teacher grading (B) | Peer grading (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pros | Fast; students see every mistake right away and correct it while the sim is fresh; no grading load for you | Most accurate; you see each student's thinking; best for a recorded grade | Students read someone else's reasoning; quicker than B |
| Cons | Students may mark generously or copy the key into blanks | Takes your time (about 3–5 min per packet); feedback arrives days later | Uneven grading; some students feel exposed |
| Lower the risk by | Requiring a different pen color, spot-checking, and grading for completion plus corrections rather than raw accuracy | Grading only the calculation and analysis items closely | Anonymous swaps (numbers instead of names); you spot-check |

**A sensible default:** self-grade (A) for the practice grade, then use the exit ticket or a short quiz for the recorded grade.

## Common misconceptions and how the sim helps

| Misconception | What to do in the sim |
|---|---|
| Melting or boiling a molecular substance breaks the covalent bonds inside the molecules. | Topic 3.2 Screen 4 (“Do not break the molecule”): melting overcomes attractions between molecules; the molecules stay intact (ice stays H₂O). |
| Any molecule with H and F (or O, N) can hydrogen bond. | Topic 3.1 Screen 4: fluoromethane has H and F but no H–F bond, so the sim shows dipole-dipole, not hydrogen bonding. |
| Doubling the temperature doubles the speed of gas particles. | Topic 3.5 Screens 3–4: average KE doubles (4.99 → 9.98 kJ/mol), but v_rms rises by √2 (517 → 731 m/s). |
| Solutions with the same molarity contain the same number of particles. | Topic 3.7 Screen 5: 250 mL and 750 mL of 0.500 M hold 0.125 and 0.375 mol. |
| A photon with part of the needed energy causes part of a transition. | Topic 3.12 Screen 4: a 600 nm photon (3.31 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) is rejected by a 4.09 × 10⁻¹⁹ J gap; the level does not move at all. |

## Sim cautions: tell students before they hit these

These are places where the sim's text or numbers can confuse students. The worksheet already works around them; mention them during the demo.

- Topic 3.4: switching the pressure unit (atm → kPa) does not convert a pressure you typed; it just relabels it. Retype the value after switching.
- Topic 3.1 writes “deg C” instead of °C, and its “scores” (dispersion score, force score) are made-up model numbers. Use them for ranking only.
- Topic 3.1 Screen 3's default pair (chloromethane + methane) shows Dispersion as the dominant force. Have students pick chloromethane + chloromethane to see dipole-dipole.
- Topic 3.9: every reset loads a new chromatography sample and reshuffles which dye letter is most polar. Record sample 1 first.
- Topic 3.8: “Represented” particle counts are rounded from the target you type (6 at model volume 2 shows 3).
- Topic 3.3: the gas sliders use relative “T” and “V” units, not kelvins or liters.
- Topic 3.10: for iodine, the words “moderate” (ethanol) and “appreciable” (hexane) don't match the order of the sim's internal scores. Focus on the explanation, not the word.
- Different topics use slightly different R values (0.08206, 0.082057366, 8.314462618). Answers agree to 3 significant figures.

## Exit ticket (last day)

**1.** Day 1: Methanol (CH₃OH) boils at 64.7 °C and methane (CH₄) at −161.5 °C. Name the force responsible and the structural feature that allows it.

*Answer:* Hydrogen bonding, because methanol has an H bonded directly to O (an O–H donor) and an O acceptor.

**2.** A 2.00 L flask holds 0.100 mol of gas at 300 K. Using R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K), what is P? If the temperature rises to 450 K at fixed V, what is P?

*Answer:* P = (0.100)(0.08206)(300)/2.00 = 1.23 atm. At 450 K: 1.23 × 450/300 = 1.85 atm.

**3.** Day 6: A solution of Allura Red (ε = 26,807 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ at 504 nm) gives A = 0.536 in a 1.00 cm cuvette. What is its concentration?

*Answer:* c = 0.536/(26,807 × 1.00) = 2.00 × 10⁻⁵ M = 20.0 µM.

## AP-style practice tied to the sim

**1.** Multiple choice: Which sample has the greatest average molecular speed? (A) N₂ at 300 K (B) N₂ at 600 K (C) CO₂ at 600 K (D) He at 300 K. Use v_rms = √(3RT/M).

*Answer:* (D) He at 300 K: 1367 m/s, vs 517 (A), 731 (B) and 583 m/s (C). Small M matters more than the doubled T.

**2.** FRQ: 1.00 mol of CO₂ is held at 320 K in a rigid 1.00 L container. (a) Calculate the pressure predicted by the ideal gas law. (b) The measured pressure is 23.8 atm. Calculate Z and say whether the pressure is higher or lower than ideal. (c) Explain the deviation at the particle level. (d) Predict whether Z gets closer to 1 if the volume is increased to 10.0 L, and justify.

*Answer:* (a) P = (1.00)(0.08206)(320)/1.00 = 26.3 atm. (b) Z = 23.8/26.3 = 0.907; lower than ideal. (c) Attractions between CO₂ molecules pull particles away from the wall, so they hit it with less force and P is lower; at this density this effect is larger than the particle-volume effect. (d) Yes: at 10.0 L the particles are far apart, attractions and particle volume matter less, so Z is near 1 (the sim gives 0.991).

**3.** FRQ: A student makes Allura Red standards at 504 nm in a 1.00 cm cuvette and gets A = 0.335 at 12.5 µM and 0.670 at 25.0 µM. (a) What is the slope of A vs c, in per µM? (b) An unknown reads A = 0.402. Find c. (c) The student repeats the unknown in a 2.00 cm cuvette. Predict A. (d) Why would measuring at 600 nm give a less precise result?

*Answer:* (a) 0.0268 per µM (εb). (b) 0.402/0.0268 = 15.0 µM. (c) A doubles to 0.804. (d) ε at 600 nm is only about 1.3 × 10³, so A is small (≈0.02) and changes little with c; errors are a larger fraction of the reading.
