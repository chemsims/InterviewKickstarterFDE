# AP Chemistry Unit 1: Atomic Structure and Properties — Simulation Lesson Plan

Simulation lesson plan · Atomic Structure Sim · 6 class periods of about 50 minutes · Worksheet: “Atomic Structure and Properties” Student Notes + Answer Key

## At a glance

| AP topics | 1.1 Moles and Molar Mass; 1.2 Mass Spectroscopy of Elements; 1.3 Elemental Composition of Pure Substances; 1.4 Composition of Mixtures; 1.5 Atomic Structure and Electron Configuration; 1.6 Photoelectron Spectroscopy; 1.7 Periodic Trends; 1.8 Valence Electrons and Ionic Compounds |
|---|---|
| Simulation | Atomic Structure Sim — chemsims.app/sims/unit1 → Unit 1 Atomic Structure and Properties → Topics 1.1–1.8 (each has Guided mode and Free Explore) |
| Big idea | How do the masses, counts and arrangement of particles in atoms explain the composition of substances, the periodic trends and the formulas of ionic compounds? |
| Time | 6 periods × ~50 min (1 teacher-demo day, 4 student sim days, 1 grading and wrap-up day) |
| Student product | The paper worksheet, filled in by hand like a lab record, then graded |

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- Convert between mass, moles and particles with n = m/M and N = n × N_A (1.1).
- Read a mass spectrum and calculate an average atomic mass as a weighted average (1.2).
- Find an empirical formula from mass data and a molecular formula from molar mass (1.3).
- Use elemental composition to decide whether a sample is pure or a mixture (1.4).
- Write ground-state electron configurations and relate them to Coulomb attraction (1.5).
- Interpret PES peak height and position, including for ions (1.6).
- Explain atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity trends with Zeff and shells (1.7).
- Predict ion charges and ionic formulas from valence electrons (1.8).

### Before day 1 (about 20 minutes)

- Run the whole sim yourself once with the Answer Key beside you, so the numbers you see match it.
- Run Topic 1.1 Guided once on the projector; the sim opens full screen. Know where Next, the Substance buttons and the step-6 mass box are.

## How this unit runs

**In short:** you show the sim first, students then work through it themselves over a few periods while they fill in the paper worksheet, and at the end the work is graded, by the students themselves or by you.

| Phase | Who drives | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Teacher demo (day 1) | Teacher at the projector | You open the sim, introduce the concept, and walk through the first part of it. Students watch, answer your questions out loud, and fill in the first worksheet page with you. |
| 2. Student sim work (days 2–5) | Students, alone or in pairs | Students run the sim themselves and fill in the worksheet as they go. They record settings, predictions, data and calculations on paper, like a lab notebook. You circulate. |
| 3. Grading and wrap-up (day 6) | Students or teacher (your choice) | Students self-grade with the answer key, or you collect and grade. Then a short class discussion and an exit ticket. |

### Paper lab-sheet norms (read these to students on day 1)

- The worksheet is your lab record. Write in pen or dark pencil, and don't erase: cross out with one line so your first thinking stays visible.
- Fill in **Set up** first and match the sim to it exactly. The answers depend on those settings.
- Write every **Predict** answer *before* you run that part of the sim. A wrong prediction loses no points; a blank one does.
- Copy numbers exactly as the sim shows them, with units.
- Show setup, numbers with units, and the answer for every **Calculate** item, then check it against the sim.
- At the bottom of each page, leave the **Self-check** score blank until grading day.

## Grading: your choice of self-grading or teacher grading

**In short:** both options use the same answer key and the point values printed on the worksheet. Self-grading is faster and makes students study their mistakes. Teacher grading is more reliable. Pick per class, or mix them.

### Point values (printed on the worksheet's last page)

Each blank or table cell = 1 point · each prediction = 1 point · each calculation = 3 points (setup, numbers with units, answer) · each analysis question and each graph = 2 points. Every page shows its own total in its **Self-check** box.

### Option A: students self-grade (about 25 minutes)

- Students put away the pen they worked in and take out a different color (green or red).
- Project the Answer Key one page at a time, or hand out printed copies to share.
- Students mark each item ✓ or ✗. For each ✗ they write the correct answer next to theirs, plus a few words on why.
- Predictions get the point if they were written down before the sim was run, right or wrong.
- Students total each page in its Self-check box, copy the totals to the Self-Grading Record on the last page, and write the question that taught them the most.
- Collect the packets. Spot-check 3–5 items per packet against the key, and initial the record.

### Option B: you grade

- Collect the packets at the end of the last sim day.
- Grade with the same key and point values. Check the calculations and analysis questions closely; the blanks go quickly.
- Hand back with the Self-Grading Record filled in. Ask students to correct their ✗ items in a second color for partial credit back (a common choice is half the lost points).

### Option C: peer grading

Students swap packets and grade with the key as in Option A, then return them; the owner writes the corrections. The grader signs the record (“A classmate”).

### Pros, cons and risks

|  | Self-grading (A) | Teacher grading (B) | Peer grading (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pros | Fast; students see every mistake right away and correct it while the sim is fresh; no grading load for you | Most accurate; you see each student's thinking; best for a recorded grade | Students read someone else's reasoning; quicker than B |
| Cons | Students may mark generously or copy the key into blanks | Takes your time (about 3–5 min per packet); feedback arrives days later | Uneven grading; some students feel exposed |
| Lower the risk by | Requiring a different pen color, spot-checking, and grading for completion plus corrections rather than raw accuracy | Grading only the calculation and analysis items closely | Anonymous swaps (numbers instead of names); you spot-check |

**A sensible default:** self-grade (A) for the practice grade, then use the exit ticket or a short quiz for the recorded grade.

## Common misconceptions and how the sim helps

| Misconception | What to do in the sim |
|---|---|
| The tallest peak in a mass spectrum is the heaviest isotope. | Topic 1.2 step 3 says “The tallest peak is not the heaviest isotope. It is the most abundant isotope.” Show ²⁰Ne (90.5%) vs ²²Ne (9.3%). |
| Mass percents can be used directly as subscripts (C₄₀H₇O₅₃). | Topic 1.3 step 3: “Masses do not directly give atom counts.” Students see 40.0 g C become 3.33 mol. |
| Equal masses mean equal numbers of particles. | Topic 1.1 step 6: 36.0 g H₂O has 1.203 × 10²⁴ molecules but 36.0 g NaCl has only 3.71 × 10²³ formula units. |
| PES shows one peak per shell. | Topic 1.6 step 2: sodium has separate 2s and 2p peaks, so 4 peaks for 3 shells. |
| Atoms with more electrons are always bigger. | Topic 1.7: Cl (17 electrons) is 79 pm but Na (11 electrons) is 190 pm; Zeff 7 vs 1. |

## Sim cautions: tell students before they hit these

These are places where the sim's text or numbers can confuse students. The worksheet already works around them; mention them during the demo.

- Topic 1.3: tell students not to move the Sample mass slider. The sim rounds every element mass each time it moves, so the percentages drift and the formula can change (CHO or CH₃O). To fix it, click Formula unit, then Molecular.
- Topic 1.1: the sim divides by the unrounded molar mass (18.015), so 36.0 g of water shows 2.00 mol but 1.203 × 10²⁴ molecules, not 1.204. Both are right to 3 significant figures.
- Topic 1.4 uses C = 12 and O = 16 exactly, but the other topics use 12.011 and 15.999. Tell students to use the numbers on that screen.
- Topic 1.6: the binding energies are model numbers, not real ones, and the peaks are evenly spaced. In overlay mode the ion's peaks sit on top of the atom's; the shift to higher energy is only in the “Ion shift” readout.
- Topic 1.5 step 2: the “Nuclear charge for comparison” slider changes the element. Put it back on 17 for chlorine.
- Topic 1.5: the sim counts Mg²⁺'s eight n = 2 electrons as “valence electrons.” Tell students many textbooks say a cation like Mg²⁺ has a noble-gas core and no valence electrons left.
- Topic 1.7 step 5: argon shows an electronegativity of “0”. That means there is no value, not that it is zero. Mg and Ar also show an electron affinity of 0.
- Topic 1.8: the pattern line says “repeat a + charge pattern” for Group 1; read it as “1+”.
- Topic 1.3 Free Explore: the first Try-it says to change the sample mass, but Free Explore only has element mass boxes. Have students double every element mass instead.

## Exit ticket (last day)

**1.** How many moles and molecules are in 9.0 g of water (M = 18.0 g/mol)?

*Answer:* 0.50 mol; 0.50 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules (the sim shows 3.009 × 10²³).

**2.** Boron is 19.9% ¹⁰B (10.013 amu) and 80.1% ¹¹B (11.009 amu). Which peak is taller, and what is the average atomic mass?

*Answer:* ¹¹B is taller (more abundant); average = 10.811 amu.

**3.** Write the formula for the compound of calcium and nitrogen and show the charge balance.

*Answer:* Ca₃N₂: 3(+2) + 2(−3) = 0.

## AP-style practice tied to the sim

**1.** Multiple choice. Na and Mg²⁺ have PES spectra. Which is true? (A) Mg²⁺ has a 3s peak. (B) Both have a 2p peak with height 6, but Mg²⁺ has one fewer peak. (C) Na has fewer peaks than Mg²⁺. (D) The 1s peak of Na is at a higher binding energy than the 1s peak of Mg²⁺.

*Answer:* (B). Na: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s (4 peaks); Mg²⁺: 1s, 2s, 2p (3 peaks); both 2p⁶. Mg²⁺ has more protons (12 vs 11) and fewer electrons, so its 1s binding energy is higher, which rules out (D).

**2.** FRQ. A 100.0 g sample of a compound contains 40.0 g C, 6.7 g H and 53.3 g O. (a) Find the moles of each element. (b) Find the empirical formula. (c) The molar mass is 180.16 g/mol; find the molecular formula. (d) A second 50.0 g sample of the same compound is analyzed. Predict its percent by mass of carbon and name the law that justifies your answer.

*Answer:* (a) C 40.0/12.011 = 3.33 mol; H 6.7/1.008 = 6.65 mol; O 53.3/15.999 = 3.33 mol. (b) ÷ 3.33 → 1 : 2 : 1, CH₂O. (c) 180.16 ÷ 30.026 = 6.00 → C₆H₁₂O₆. (d) 40.0% C; law of definite proportions (a pure compound has a fixed mass composition).
